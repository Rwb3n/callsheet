# Freemium conversion benchmarks for B2B directories and the CALLSHEET free/paid boundary

**A free listing on CALLSHEET should be genuinely useful to buyers — complete contact details, basic credits, and a functional profile — while gating visibility amplification, analytics intelligence, and trust signals behind two paid tiers that target a 5–8% conversion rate.** This recommendation draws on conversion data from over 1,000 B2B SaaS products, six major directory platforms, and detailed case studies of LinkedIn and Yelp's freemium mechanics. The benchmarks consistently show that B2B directory conversion rates cluster between 3% and 8%, that visibility is the strongest value metric for supplier-side monetisation, and that the single most dangerous mistake is gating the buyer experience behind the supplier's payment decision.

---

## 1. What the benchmarks actually say about freemium conversion

The most methodologically rigorous dataset comes from a joint survey by Lenny Rachitsky, Kyle Poyar (OpenView), and Pendo covering **1,000+ B2B SaaS products**, published August 2023. It establishes the definitive tiers:

| Model | "Good" conversion | "Great" conversion |
|---|---|---|
| Freemium self-serve | **3–5%** | **6–8%** |
| Freemium + sales-assist | **5–7%** | **10–15%** |
| Free trial (time-limited) | **8–12%** | **15–25%** |

OpenView's own 2022 benchmark report (n=450+) found a **5% median** freemium-to-paid rate, while ProfitWell's dataset spanning 73,000+ companies converged on **3–5% average** with top performers reaching 10%+. First Page Sage's longitudinal study of 80+ SaaS clients (2021–2025) measured a lower **2.6% organic** freemium-to-paid rate. The distribution is heavily right-skewed: roughly 20% of products convert below 2.5%, a third land between 2.5–5%, and only 15% exceed 20%.

For directory and marketplace models specifically, the data is thinner but convergent. Yelp — the closest publicly traded analogue — converts approximately **6.7%** of its 7.74 million claimed business pages into 515,000 paying advertiser locations. This figure has actually declined from roughly 8.8% in 2022 as Yelp's strategy shifted toward extracting more revenue per advertiser (a record **$2,707/year** in 2024) rather than maximising conversion breadth. Neither Bessemer's Cloud Index nor a16z's Marketplace 100 publish freemium conversion benchmarks specifically; a16z focuses on match rate and GMV retention as marketplace health indicators rather than supplier monetisation rates.

**For CALLSHEET's planning purposes, a realistic conversion range is 3–5% at launch with a target of 6–8% at maturity.** Very small business customers convert at the highest rates across all quartiles, and companies adding sales-assist to freemium see materially higher conversion. In the UK broadcast/film/TV production industry — a relationship-driven niche where word-of-mouth is powerful — the combination of self-serve upgrade paths and light-touch sales outreach should push toward the higher end.

## 2. How real B2B directories monetise their providers

Published conversion data from specific directory platforms is scarce — most treat these metrics as proprietary — but their business models reveal clear patterns.

**Yelp** operates the most transparent model: free listings include 20+ genuine tools (analytics, messaging, review management, quote requests), while paid tiers start at £150/month for CPC advertising and £180/month for the Upgrade Package that removes competitor ads, adds custom CTAs, and enables branded profiles. The combined package runs from £270/month. Roughly **48% of Yelp advertisers are small businesses spending under $300/month**. The critical insight is that Yelp's revenue has grown to a record **$1.41 billion** (2024) even as paying locations declined 8% over 2.5 years — proving that a niche directory doesn't need mass conversion if average revenue per paying provider is sufficient.

**Clutch.co** requires a 12-month commitment for sponsorship packages and uses algorithmic prioritisation of paid placements above organic rank. Free profiles receive organic visibility, but agencies report steep lead drops when sponsorship lapses — creating a soft lock-in effect. **Bark.com** and **Thumbtack** operate pure pay-per-lead models where the provider profile is free but buyer contact details are gated behind credit purchases (roughly £13/lead on Bark, $10–$100/lead on Thumbtack). Lead-to-job conversion on these platforms runs **10–30%**, with significant provider complaints about lead quality. **Angi/HomeAdvisor** charges a $350 annual fee plus $15–$100 per lead and achieves a **40–50% match rate** on service requests — but its market penetration remains just 4% of the $500 billion home services market.

The critical lesson from **Angie's List's history** is directly relevant to CALLSHEET: Angie's List originally charged buyers a subscription ($22+/year), which constrained buyer growth so severely that the company was forced to drop the model entirely in 2016 before merging with HomeAdvisor. **Charging buyers — even modestly — is a structural trap for directories.** The successful model across all six platforms studied is free access for buyers, monetisation from the supply side.

## 3. Which gated features actually drive upgrades

The evidence hierarchy for conversion-driving features in directory contexts, ranked by published impact, reveals that not all gates are equal.

**Analytics and engagement visibility** is the single most powerful conversion lever in the data. LinkedIn's internal surveys show **74% of Premium subscribers** cite "who viewed your profile" as their top reason for upgrading. LinkedIn's mechanic is precisely the template CALLSHEET should follow: show the count, hide the names. Free users see "X people viewed your profile this week" with only the 5 most recent names visible; the rest appear as blurred silhouettes behind a paywall. Push notifications saying "your profile appeared in 47 searches this week" create an information gap users feel compelled to close. This is directly analogous to telling CALLSHEET providers "your listing was viewed 47 times this month — upgrade to see who."

**Priority search placement** generates measurable lead volume differences. Directory platform research indicates featured listings can **triple directory-generated leads** compared to organic placement. Premium subscribers appearing at the top of search results benefit from both increased visibility and an implied endorsement ("impression of market leadership"). Category-specific featured placement outperforms general featured listings because traffic is more qualified.

**Competitor ads on free profiles** — the Trustpilot model — represent the most aggressive conversion lever available. Trustpilot displays competitor advertisements on free business profiles; paid plans (from $259/month) remove them. This creates *negative value* on the free tier rather than merely limiting positive value. It is highly effective but carries significant reputational risk in a tight-knit industry. Yelp's similar practice of showing competitor ads on free profiles is its **single most criticised tactic** — perceived by businesses as hostile ransom ("pay us or we'll send your customers to your competitors").

**Verified/trust badges** provide a moderate but consistent conversion lift. Amazon's Prime badge drives conversion rates between **15–25%**, and 87% of consumers find ads more trustworthy with trust marks even at only 4 stars. In a professional services directory, verified credentials (confirmed IMDB credits, insurance verification, compliance badges) directly affect whether a buyer sends an enquiry.

**Review solicitation tools** are a sleeper conversion lever. Trustpilot's free plan allows only 50 review invitations per month; paid plans offer unlimited. BazaarVoice's 2023 study found that when shoppers engage with reviews, there's a **144% increase in conversion rate** — making the ability to actively collect reviews a powerful growth tool worth gating.

## 4. Yelp's model offers both a blueprint and a warning

Yelp's freemium architecture is the most relevant case study for CALLSHEET because it operates the same fundamental model: free listings for providers, free search for buyers, monetisation through supplier upgrades.

The positive lesson is that Yelp's free tier is genuinely generous — 20+ tools including analytics, messaging, review response, and quote receipt — which drove **7.74 million claimed pages** and created the content mass that makes the platform valuable to consumers. Yelp built audience first and monetised second, growing to 54.5 million listings before most businesses even claimed pages. The self-serve advertising channel, which grew **15–20% year-over-year**, became Yelp's most efficient revenue source by eliminating the cost of sales teams.

The negative lessons are equally instructive. Yelp's practice of displaying competitor ads on free profiles generated the dominant criticism that its model feels coercive. Over **2,045 complaints** were filed with the FTC, a class-action lawsuit reached the 9th Circuit (Levitt v. Yelp, 2014), and a documentary — *Billion Dollar Bully* — featured business owners alleging extortion. While the FTC closed its investigation without action, and the 9th Circuit ruled that "threatening economic harm to induce a person to pay for a legitimate service is not extortion," the reputational damage was severe and permanent. Yelp's opaque review-filtering algorithm — which hides roughly 25% of submitted reviews without publicly disclosing criteria — became the lightning rod for allegations that positive reviews disappeared after businesses refused advertising. Independent research from Harvard Business School found no systematic bias toward advertisers, but the *perception* of manipulation proved impossible to dispel.

**For a niche UK production directory, the margin for error is zero.** The broadcast/film/TV production industry is small, relationship-driven, and word travels fast. A single widely-shared negative experience from a respected DoP or line producer could kill adoption. CALLSHEET must learn from Yelp's content: never display competitor profiles on a business's own listing page, make any algorithmic sorting fully transparent, and maintain absolute separation between editorial integrity (reviews, recommendations) and commercial relationships.

## 5. LinkedIn's data-teasing playbook is CALLSHEET's best conversion template

LinkedIn has built a **$2 billion annual subscription business** (Premium Career + Business alone, as of January 2025) by strategically controlling the flow of engagement visibility data. Premium subscribers grew **50% in two years**, and Premium sign-ups increased 51% in fiscal year 2024. The implied conversion rate across LinkedIn's 1.2 billion registered users is roughly **15–18%**, though this is inflated by enterprise team purchases and the inclusion of Sales Navigator and Recruiter tiers.

LinkedIn's conversion architecture operates through five reinforcing mechanisms. First, the **blurred profile viewer list** — free users see "X people viewed your profile" with only 5 recent names visible, while Premium unlocks the full list for 365 days with demographic filtering. Second, **search appearance notifications** — emails saying "your profile appeared in 47 searches this week" with aggregate data on searcher demographics but specific identities locked behind Premium. Third, **time-limited data windows** — free users see only 90 days of limited history versus 365 days for Premium. Fourth, **progressive feature restriction** — LinkedIn recently reduced free personalised connection invitations to just 5 per month and capped search results at 10 pages, explicitly to drive Premium conversion. Fifth, **free trials with credit card required** that create the endowment effect: users experience full data access, become accustomed to it, then face losing it.

The psychological stack underlying LinkedIn's approach combines **FOMO** ("someone important viewed your profile — but who?"), **loss aversion** ("you're missing opportunities by not seeing who's interested"), and **social proof** (the gold Premium badge visible to others). The critical mechanic for CALLSHEET is that LinkedIn is essentially charging users to see *their own engagement data* — data that LinkedIn generates and controls. This is precisely what CALLSHEET can replicate: "your listing was viewed 47 times this month by production companies — upgrade to see which ones."

However, LinkedIn's approach sits at the boundary of legitimate conversion and manipulative design. The platform has been documented on Deceptive Patterns (formerly DarkPatterns.org) for forced continuity, nagging, and button manipulation during cancellation. **65% of Premium subscribers renew after the first year**, but monthly subscriber churn reaches 19% after 3 months, suggesting a meaningful segment that tries and abandons. CALLSHEET should adopt the data-teasing mechanic but avoid dark patterns in cancellation — month-to-month billing with easy, one-click cancellation will build trust in a small industry.

## 6. Visibility is the right value metric; analytics is a trap

Patrick Campbell's framework from ProfitWell defines a value metric as "how a customer pays you, and what they pay for" — the unit of pricing that aligns with perceived value. A good value metric passes four tests: it aligns with customer value, it's easy to digest, it grows with usage, and the customer *wants* it to increase even if it means paying more.

For CALLSHEET, **visibility** — how many buyers see and engage with your listing — is the primary value metric. It aligns directly with why suppliers join (to be found by production companies), it's intuitive ("your profile was seen by 47 production companies"), it scales naturally as the platform grows, and suppliers unambiguously want more of it. This mirrors LinkedIn's approach (charging for enhanced visibility in search), Clutch's model (paying for algorithmic priority), and Yelp's CPC advertising (paying for more eyeballs).

**Profile richness** (showreel hosting, unlimited credits, media galleries) works well as a feature gate between tiers but is a weak primary value metric because it has a ceiling — once a profile is "complete," there's no natural expansion revenue. **Trust** (verification badges, confirmed credits) is an excellent premium add-on but binary rather than continuously scaling. 

**Analytics and intelligence** should be treated cautiously as a value metric. Campbell himself warned that "analytics products are terrible — willingness to pay is terrible; retention is terrible; NPS is terrible." ProfitWell pivoted its own analytics product to freemium specifically because no one would pay for graphs. Analytics works as a *conversion lever* (showing teasers to drive upgrades) but not as the *primary thing people pay for*. The paying supplier is paying for visibility and leads; the analytics are the proof of value, not the value itself.

The optimal architecture, per Price Intelligently's research, gates roughly **20% of high-value features** behind payment while offering 80% of functionality free. For CALLSHEET, this means the free profile should be a complete, dignified listing that helps buyers — with the 20% reserved for visibility amplification, trust signals, and intelligence tools that help providers compete more effectively.

## 7. The first enquiry within seven days is CALLSHEET's activation event

Time-to-value data is unambiguous: **users who fail to activate in the first few days rarely convert later**. Gartner research, confirmed by Mixpanel platform data, shows that users engaging with core features within the first week are **5x more likely to convert** to paid. Nearly half of all freemium users vanish within 30 days, and most conversions occur within the first 30 days with diminishing returns after 90.

The average SaaS activation rate is **36%** (Rachitsky/Timen), with the best companies using 3-step onboarding tours that achieve **72% completion** versus 7-step tours that collapse to 16% (Chameleon.io). Famous activation benchmarks from analogous platforms include Facebook's "7 friends in 10 days," Slack's "2,000 team messages" (after which 93% stick), and Grubhub's finding that **conversion rate doubles** when users see 55+ restaurant results after entering their address.

For a B2B directory, the provider-side activation event is receiving the first enquiry or demonstrable evidence of buyer engagement. While no directory has published the exact multiplier, the pattern is universal across marketplace literature: providers who receive their first lead within the first week convert at dramatically higher rates than those who don't. CALLSHEET's onboarding must therefore prioritise two things: (1) getting providers to complete a minimum viable profile within the first session (3 steps maximum — name/category, contact details, 1 credit/project), and (2) delivering evidence of buyer engagement within 7 days — even if that means showing "your profile appeared in X buyer searches this week" rather than actual enquiries during the cold-start phase.

Companies running **10+ conversion optimisation experiments per quarter** achieve 60% higher conversion than industry average (ProfitWell, 1,000+ companies). Those using Product Qualified Leads (PQLs) — identifying free users whose behaviour signals readiness to buy — convert at **3x the rate** of those without PQL tracking (25% vs. 8%). Only 25% of product-led companies currently use PQLs, suggesting this is a massive untapped opportunity for CALLSHEET to implement from day one.

## 8. Keep provider contact free for buyers; gate buyer details from providers

The evidence on contact information gating is clear and directional. Across all six major directory platforms studied, the dominant pattern is **asymmetric gating**: provider contact details are freely visible to buyers, while buyer enquiry details are monetised from providers.

Yelp, Clutch, Houzz, and Angi all show provider contact information freely — phone numbers, websites, addresses, and email — because reducing buyer friction increases enquiry volume, which increases the value proposition that drives provider upgrades. Bark and Thumbtack represent the alternative model where buyer contact details are gated behind credit purchases from providers, but even these platforms show provider profiles freely to buyers.

**Gating provider contact details from buyers is rare, counterproductive, and should be avoided entirely.** Angie's List's history is the definitive case study: charging buyers even $22/year created friction that constrained buyer growth so severely the model had to be abandoned. General content-gating research shows businesses **lose 93% of potential visitors** when content is put behind a paywall. In a B2B production directory, buyers (production managers, heads of department) expect to find and contact providers freely. If CALLSHEET gates provider phone numbers or emails behind payment, buyers will simply use Google, IMDB Pro, or industry contacts instead.

The correct approach for CALLSHEET is to show full provider contact details to buyers on all listing tiers, then monetise providers through: (a) enhanced visibility in search results, (b) analytics on who viewed and engaged with their profile, (c) priority placement and featured listing positions, and (d) trust badges and portfolio tools that increase their win rate when buyers do find them. The provider is paying for competitive advantage, not for the basic right to be contactable.

## 9. How directories get the free/paid line fatally wrong

Three failure modes recur across case studies, each with direct implications for CALLSHEET.

**Giving too much away free** is the most common failure. Equals, a next-gen spreadsheet tool, launched a generous free plan in November 2022 after successfully growing with gated, sales-led access. Within six months, the business stalled completely — free workspaces wouldn't convert because users got full value without ever hitting a conversion trigger. CEO Bobby Pinero publicly called freemium "a decision that broke our business" and killed the free plan entirely. Evernote offered 100,000 free notes for years, building a massive but non-paying user base; the company was unprofitable for years before being acquired by Bending Spoons in a fire sale. Codecademy waited four years to monetise and admitted "we could've and should've monetised sooner — diminishing returns providing a free product." The rule: **when you solve a complete problem completely for free, you eliminate your monetisation opportunity.**

**Being too aggressive with paywalls** prevents critical mass. The Times (London) implemented a hard paywall in 2010 and recruited only 105,000 paying visitors. Dating apps that deliberately degrade the free experience to frustrate users into paying have generated significant backlash and trust erosion. A documented marketplace case pitched a $1/day boost model to investors — the revenue was capped at (number of suppliers × $1), missing the exponential value in actual transactions. Directory builder Connor Finlayson documented that "most directories fail" because founders "set up a monetization strategy, wait for the money to roll in… and nothing happens." **Paywalling before traffic exists is fatal.**

**Changing the free/paid line after launch** triggers the most visceral backlash. Evernote's 2023 reduction from 100,000 free notes to 50 notes and 1 notebook generated user hostility described as "data hostage-taking" — years of notes locked behind a new paywall, driving mass migration to Notion and Obsidian. Price increases of 70%+ for existing paid users compounded the damage. LinkedIn's late-2024 reduction of free personalised connection invitations to just 5 per month was widely viewed as a forced-upgrade tactic. The behavioural economics explanation is the **zero-price effect**: consumers are irrationally resistant to charges after prolonged free use because setting a price of $0 trains customers to perceive the service as worth nothing. New Relic managed a successful pricing transition by grandfathering existing customers gradually; HubSpot avoided backlash by framing changes as value improvements. **CALLSHEET must set its free/paid line correctly from V1 because moving it later is extremely costly.**

## 10. Recommended free/paid boundary for CALLSHEET V1

Based on the full body of benchmark data, case studies, and conversion mechanics analysed above, the following tier structure balances buyer utility, provider conversion incentive, and anti-pattern avoidance.

### Free tier (the buyer-satisfying base)

The free listing must be complete enough that a production manager searching CALLSHEET can find, evaluate, and contact a provider without friction. It should include:

- Full company/individual name and contact details (phone, email, website)
- Business description, service categories, and location/availability regions
- Up to 5 portfolio items or credits (enough to demonstrate capability)
- Appear in search results (standard organic ranking)
- Receive and respond to buyer enquiries (unlimited)
- Basic profile analytics: total view count as a number ("your listing was viewed X times this month") — **but not who viewed it**

This approach follows the 80/20 rule from Price Intelligently research and mirrors Yelp's genuinely useful free tier that drove 7.74 million claimed pages. The free listing is dignified, not degraded.

### Tier 1 — "Professional" (£29–49/month, primary conversion lever)

The first paid tier should gate **visibility amplification** and **engagement intelligence** — the features with the highest proven conversion impact:

- **Full analytics dashboard**: See which companies/buyers viewed your profile, search terms that found you, and weekly trend data (the LinkedIn playbook — 74% cite this as top upgrade reason)
- **Enhanced search ranking**: Priority placement in relevant buyer searches (featured listings triple directory-generated leads)
- **Unlimited portfolio items**: Full showreel hosting, unlimited credits, image galleries, PDF uploads
- **Verified badge**: "CALLSHEET Verified" trust signal confirming credentials, insurance, or reference checks
- **Review solicitation tools**: Ability to proactively request reviews/endorsements from past collaborators
- **Availability calendar**: Show real-time availability to buyers (reduces wasted enquiries)

The conversion trigger should be analytics-based: "Your listing was viewed 47 times this month by 12 different production companies — upgrade to Professional to see which ones and appear higher in their searches." This notification should fire within the first 7 days after profile creation, even if the numbers are modest.

### Tier 2 — "Featured" (£79–129/month, upsell for active providers)

The premium tier adds **maximum visibility** and **competitive intelligence** for providers who are already getting value from Tier 1:

- **Featured placement**: Appear in "Recommended" sections, category spotlights, and homepage featured listings
- **Competitor benchmarking**: See how your profile views, enquiry rate, and response time compare to category averages
- **AI matching priority**: First to appear in intelligent buyer-provider matching recommendations
- **Branded profile**: Custom header, logo prominence, and styled layout (no generic template)
- **Direct booking integration**: Allow buyers to check availability and send booking requests through the platform
- **Multi-user access**: Team members can manage the listing (relevant for larger facilities/companies)

### Expected conversion rates

| Metric | Conservative | Target | Optimistic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free → Tier 1 | 3% | 5–6% | 8% |
| Tier 1 → Tier 2 | 15% | 20–25% | 30% |
| Overall paid rate | 3.5% | 6% | 10% |
| Time to first conversion | 30–60 days | 14–30 days | 7–14 days |

These ranges are grounded in the Lenny/Poyar benchmark of 3–5% "good" for self-serve freemium, Yelp's 6.7% across a mature platform, and the uplift from sales-assist (which CALLSHEET should add selectively for high-value providers like post-production houses and equipment rental companies).

### Activation triggers to implement from day one

- **Day 0**: After profile creation, show immediate data: "Your listing is now visible to X production companies in your category" (even if estimated from buyer search volume)
- **Day 3**: Email with first engagement data: "Your profile appeared in Y searches this week"  
- **Day 7**: The critical window — if the provider has received an enquiry, trigger a "see who's viewing you" upgrade prompt. If no enquiry, send curated buyer activity data showing demand in their category
- **Day 14**: LinkedIn-style blurred data email: "3 production companies viewed your full profile this week — upgrade to see who" with a contextual offer (first month free or 20% discount)
- **Day 30**: If not converted, shift to social proof: "47 [category] providers in your region have upgraded to Professional this month"

### Anti-patterns CALLSHEET must avoid

- **Never show competitor listings on a provider's own profile page.** Yelp's and Trustpilot's most criticised tactic. In a small industry, this will generate immediate, vocal backlash.
- **Never gate provider contact details from buyers.** Angie's List proved buyer friction is fatal. Production managers must be able to find and contact providers freely.
- **Never use opaque algorithmic ranking without explanation.** Yelp's review-filtering opacity fuelled extortion allegations for a decade. Clearly label "Featured" results and explain organic ranking factors.
- **Never use aggressive outbound sales.** Yelp's documented 20+ calls per week to single businesses would be catastrophic in a tight-knit production community. Use in-app prompts, email sequences, and case studies instead.
- **Set the free/paid line correctly at launch and don't move it.** Evernote's experience shows that reducing free features post-launch triggers "data hostage" backlash. If anything changes, grandfather existing users and add value rather than removing it.
- **Don't over-rely on analytics as the primary value proposition.** Per Patrick Campbell: "Analytics products have terrible willingness-to-pay." Use analytics as the *conversion lever* (the teaser that drives upgrades) but position the paid tier as being about visibility and competitive advantage.
- **Don't launch monetisation before demonstrating demand.** The directory must have enough buyer traffic to deliver real engagement data to providers. Premature monetisation before critical mass will produce the "most directories fail" outcome: nobody visits, nobody signs up, no money comes in.

### The core strategic principle

CALLSHEET's free tier exists to serve buyers and build the content that makes the platform valuable. The paid tiers exist to serve providers' competitive ambitions. The free/paid line should never compromise the buyer experience — every restriction should be on the provider side, gating tools that help providers *compete better* rather than restricting what buyers can see or do. The conversion engine is engagement data: every profile view, search appearance, and enquiry is both a proof of platform value and a prompt to upgrade. Show the number, gate the detail, and let the data sell the subscription.
