# On-screen talent scope decision for CALLSHEET

**The exclusion of individual on-screen talent profiles from CALLSHEET V1 is strategically correct — but the current scope has a blind spot.** Excluding actors is unambiguously right: buyers never search production directories for actors, and competing with Spotlight's 99% penetration would be futile. However, excluding voice-over agencies, presenter agencies, and similar talent-adjacent B2B services creates a competitive gap against 4rfv, which returns results for these searches. The optimal V1 position is not "exclude all talent" but rather "include all talent-facing B2B services while excluding individual performer profiles" — with architecture designed to accommodate individual talent profiles in V2+ if market demand materialises. GDPR compliance for talent data is manageable, not prohibitive. The biggest risk is not exclusion but premature inclusion, which every comparable platform expansion has handled poorly.

---

## 1. The UK casting ecosystem runs on Spotlight — and Spotlight alone

**Spotlight is a near-monopoly.** Founded in 1927 and claiming **99% of UK professional TV productions** are cast through its platform, Spotlight operates as the industry's essential infrastructure. Approximately **65,000–70,000 performers** maintain profiles at **£216/year**, with agents accessing the platform free and casting directors paying £70–£245 depending on subscription length. Every major UK broadcaster (BBC, ITV, Channel 4) and theatre company (National Theatre, RSC) casts through Spotlight. The industry phrase "if you're not in Spotlight, people think you're not working or you're dead" captures the platform's stranglehold.

Critically, **Spotlight and Casting Networks are owned by the same parent company, Talent Systems**, which also owns Staff Me Up (crew hiring), Tagmin (agent management software used by 500+ UK agencies), and Cast It (studio casting decisions). This means a single corporate entity controls the entire UK digital casting infrastructure. Casting Networks itself is primarily a US commercial casting platform with limited UK penetration, used mainly for commercials rather than drama. Its UK presence is secondary and supplementary to Spotlight.

Spotlight also operates a **"Contacts" directory** — a B2B industry address book covering 100+ categories of production services, from casting directors and agents to accountants, photographers, and set construction companies. Basic listings are free; enhanced listings cost £480/year. This directory is relatively basic compared to modern B2B platforms, but its existence means Spotlight already occupies adjacent territory to what CALLSHEET is building.

**What this means for CALLSHEET:** Any attempt to include individual actor profiles would compete directly with a monopoly-strength incumbent backed by significant capital. This is not a market gap — it is a fortified position. The actor-casting workflow (production → casting director → Spotlight → agent → talent) is deeply entrenched, relationship-driven, and adequately served.

## 2. Voice-over artists and presenters are the real edge case

Not all on-screen talent is sourced through the Spotlight pipeline. **Voice-over artists and presenters operate as freelance service providers**, sourced through fundamentally different B2B workflows that overlap with production services procurement.

**Voice-over artists** are hired without casting directors in most corporate, commercial, and e-learning contexts. Buyers contact specialist voice agencies (Qvoice, Soho Voices, Matinée), receive demo clips, select a voice, and receive a delivered audio file — functionally identical to hiring a sound studio or post-production house. Online marketplaces (Voices.com, Voquent, Voice123) treat voice work as a freelance service transaction. Rates start at approximately **£370/hour** through agencies.

**Presenters** for corporate video, events, and branded content are similarly sourced through specialist agencies (Great British Presenters, Speak-easy) or booked directly. TV presenters for broadcast work through talent agents, but the corporate/commercial presenter market bypasses the traditional casting pipeline entirely. Many presenters now operate from home green-screen studios, positioning them as remote-bookable service providers.

**The competitive intelligence is decisive here.** 4rfv, CALLSHEET's direct Belfast-based competitor with ~4,700 listings, includes categories for "Television Presenters," "Voice Talent – Independent" (with individual voice artist profiles), and 12+ categories of talent agents and casting services. The Knowledge takes a more conservative approach — listing voice-over agencies and casting directors as B2B entities but not individual performers. Both competitors include casting directors and talent agents.

If a production coordinator planning a corporate video searches CALLSHEET for "voice-over" or "presenter" and gets zero results, **this creates a competitive gap, not just a credibility gap**. The same buyer gets results on 4rfv. For actors, buyers already know to go to Spotlight. For voice and presenter services, buyers expect to find these alongside other production services.

## 3. How competitor directories draw the talent boundary

The competitive landscape reveals three distinct approaches to the talent question:

**The Knowledge (theknowledgeonline.com)** represents the conservative B2B model. With **15,000+ entries across ~500 categories**, it explicitly positions itself as covering "crew, service providers, suppliers and anyone who may work behind the camera in film & TV." It includes 117 talent agency listings, 74 casting director listings, and voice-over agency listings — but zero individual performer profiles. Enhanced listings run **£495–£1,120/year**. The Knowledge has operated successfully with this boundary for nearly 40 years, and no user complaints about the exclusion of individual talent were found. This validates the B2B-only model.

**4rfv.co.uk** takes a more inclusive approach, featuring individual voice talent listings and TV presenter listings alongside its 32 main directory categories. With **~110,000 unique visitors** and 831 listings under "Casting and Agents" alone, 4rfv demonstrates that a production directory can include talent-adjacent categories without losing its B2B identity. Its inclusion of "Voice Talent – Independent" and "Stunt Coordinators & Performers" as individual listings goes beyond The Knowledge's B2B-entity-only approach.

**ProductionHub (productionhub.com)** fully integrates talent, with an extensive "Talent / Casting / Agencies" section covering actors, voice-over artists, commercial talent, hosts, and more — all using the same profile infrastructure as crew and vendor listings. With **150,000+ profiles across 300+ categories**, ProductionHub proves the combined model is technically feasible. However, ProductionHub is heavily US-focused, and its talent profiles lack the depth of dedicated casting platforms (no playing age ranges, limited physical attribute filtering, no showreel organisation by credit type).

**Mandy.com** offers the most cautionary example. Originally two platforms — Casting Call Pro (talent) and Mandy.com (crew) — they merged into The Mandy Network and were subsequently acquired by Backstage. The combined platform claims **2.9 million registered members** but user sentiment has collapsed: **Trustpilot reviews average ~3.4 stars**, with dominant complaints about quality dilution, broken filtering between talent and crew categories, and profile data loss after the merger. Talent and crew profiles use different templates but the same subscription price (~£20/month UK). Critically, subscriptions are type-locked — performer subscribers cannot access crew listings, and vice versa. The "combined" model is largely a branding exercise rather than genuine integration.

## 4. No platform has successfully bolted talent onto a crew directory

The platform expansion evidence is unequivocal. **No B2B production services directory has successfully added individual talent profiles as a V2/V3 expansion within a single platform.** Every successful combination of talent and crew has been achieved through acquisition of separate brands maintained independently.

**Talent Systems' "portfolio of brands" strategy** is the industry's most successful model. Rather than merging Casting Networks (talent), Spotlight (talent), and Staff Me Up (crew) into a single platform, each brand maintains its own identity, user base, and interface. This avoids the quality dilution and user confusion that plagued Mandy's forced integration.

**Mandy/Casting Call Pro** is the primary cautionary tale. The merger of a respected UK casting platform (CCP) with a crew-focused platform (Mandy) under a single brand caused significant user dissatisfaction. Long-time users report that "the rot initially set in when Casting Call Pro became Mandy," and the subsequent Backstage acquisition compounded the problem through profile syncing that caused data loss. Users who had built profiles over **24 years** reported their credits and work history vanishing.

**Stage 32** succeeded as an all-in-one platform for film professionals (500,000–1,000,000+ members), but only because it positioned itself as a networking and education hub rather than a hiring or casting directory. Its revenue comes from webinars and classes, not from connecting buyers with talent or crew.

**The architectural lesson is clear:** talent profiles and crew/service profiles have fundamentally different data models (headshots vs. equipment lists, playing age vs. day rate, physical attributes vs. technical certifications), serve different buyer workflows (casting director searches vs. production manager procurement), and attract different decision-makers. Combining them in a single platform requires either maintaining separate profile templates and search interfaces (which undermines the simplicity argument) or forcing both into a unified template (which serves neither well).

## 5. GDPR is manageable — not a blocker

Talent profiles containing ethnicity, disability status, and potentially gender identity constitute **special category personal data under UK GDPR Article 9**. This requires a higher compliance bar than standard personal data, but the burden is routine and well-precedented.

**The classification is clear.** Ethnicity is definitively special category data ("data revealing racial or ethnic origin"). Disability status is definitively health data. Gender identity is **not automatically** special category per ICO guidance, but should be treated as such as a precaution. Age, height, eye colour, and general physical measurements are standard personal data, not special category.

**Explicit consent under Article 9(2)(a) is the appropriate legal basis.** When talent voluntarily create public profiles and provide their own ethnicity and disability data, this is the paradigm case for explicit consent — genuine choice, clear benefit to the data subject, and easy withdrawal. The **"manifestly made public" exemption (Article 9(2)(e))** provides a strong supplementary basis, since talent deliberately publish this data for industry professionals to find.

**Practical compliance requirements** include: a Data Protection Impact Assessment (almost certainly required), granular consent flows separate from Terms of Service, data subject rights mechanisms (access, erasure, portability), an appropriate policy document, and potentially a Data Protection Officer if processing at scale. The **estimated setup cost is £5,000–£15,000** for legal/privacy consultancy plus 3–4 weeks of development for privacy architecture.

**Multiple UK platforms already process this data at scale** — Spotlight, Casting Networks, Mandy, and The Casting Collective all handle ethnicity, disability status, and physical characteristics under UK GDPR without enforcement action. The ICO has not taken action against any casting or talent directory platform. An **ICO audit of AI recruitment tools in November 2024** made ~300 recommendations around filtering by protected characteristics and data retention, signalling active regulatory interest in the recruitment technology space — but the findings were about improving practices, not shutting platforms down.

**Bottom line:** GDPR adds approximately **£10,000–£20,000 in total first-year cost** (legal setup + development + ongoing compliance) to the talent profile feature. This is a standard cost of doing business, not a reason to exclude talent.

## 6. The UK talent market is large but mostly locked up

**Equity** (the UK performers' union) has approximately **50,000 members** spanning actors, singers, dancers, variety artists, and stage managers. **Spotlight** lists **60,000–70,000 performers**. The broader DCMS "music, performing and visual arts" subsector encompasses **311,000 jobs**, though this includes musicians, visual artists, and non-screen roles. Background artists (extras) represent an estimated **100,000–200,000 registered individuals** across specialist agencies, though with significant double-counting.

The realistic addressable market for CALLSHEET's talent profiles — assuming a flat-fee subscription model — is concentrated in categories underserved by Spotlight:

- **Presenters/hosts** (corporate, events, digital): estimated 3,000–5,000 in the UK
- **Voice-over artists**: estimated 3,000–7,000 professionals
- **Commercial talent** and multi-hyphenate creatives: estimated 5,000–10,000
- **Stunt performers**: approximately 300–400 on the British Stunt Register

**Professional actors would be extremely difficult to attract.** They already pay £216/year for Spotlight (essential) and potentially £10–20/month for Mandy or Backstage (supplementary). A third subscription is a hard sell unless CALLSHEET offers unique value — specifically, visibility to production companies already using the platform for crew and services.

At a £15–25/month subscription, a conservative estimate of **3,000–5,000 talent subscribers** would generate **£720K–£1.5M ARR**. An optimistic scenario of 10,000–15,000 subscribers could reach £2.4–£4.5M ARR. These are meaningful revenue numbers but carry significant customer acquisition cost and product complexity trade-offs.

## 7. Decision framework: four options evaluated

### Option A — Exclude talent entirely from all versions

| Dimension | Assessment |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Development cost** | Lowest. No talent-specific features to build. |
| **Data protection burden** | Minimal. Standard personal data only for crew/service profiles. |
| **Competitive positioning** | Defensible against The Knowledge (same approach) but creates a gap against 4rfv, which includes voice talent and presenter categories. |
| **Addressable market impact** | Foregoes £720K–£4.5M potential ARR from talent. More importantly, excludes voice-over and presenter agencies that buyers expect to find alongside production services. |
| **Scope creep risk** | Zero. Clear boundary. |
| **Verdict** | **Too restrictive.** Permanently excluding even talent agencies and voice-over agencies leaves CALLSHEET less useful than 4rfv for common production workflows. This option conflates "no individual performer profiles" with "no talent-adjacent categories at all." |

### Option B — Include casting directors and talent agents only (current V1 plan)

| Dimension | Assessment |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Development cost** | Low. These are standard B2B service provider profiles — no special template needed. |
| **Data protection burden** | Low. Business contact data, not personal performer data. No special category data. |
| **Competitive positioning** | Matches The Knowledge's approach. Partially competitive with 4rfv but still missing voice-over agencies and presenter agencies as explicit categories. |
| **Addressable market impact** | Captures the B2B intermediary layer. Misses direct voice-over and presenter service listings that 4rfv includes. |
| **Scope creep risk** | Low-moderate. Clear boundary but may face pressure to expand categories when users search for "voice-over" and get limited results. |
| **Verdict** | **Good starting point but needs category expansion.** The V1 taxonomy should be audited to ensure voice-over agencies, presenter agencies, extras agencies, and stunt coordinators are included as B2B service categories under the existing framework — not as talent profiles, but as service providers. This mirrors The Knowledge's model while closing the gap with 4rfv. |

### Option C — Include talent in V1 with separate profile template and data model

| Dimension | Assessment |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Development cost** | High. Requires a separate profile template (headshots, showreels, physical attributes, playing age, skills/accents), separate search filters (ethnicity, height, age range, gender), and separate buyer UX. Estimated 8–12 additional weeks of development. |
| **Data protection burden** | Significant. Requires DPIA, granular consent architecture, special category data handling, potentially a DPO. £10–20K additional first-year compliance cost. |
| **Competitive positioning** | Would compete directly with Spotlight (99% penetration) and Mandy (declining but established). Differentiation unclear. Risk of being perceived as a "jack of all trades, master of none." |
| **Addressable market impact** | Potentially 3,000–15,000 talent subscribers at £15–25/month. But acquiring these users requires marketing spend and talent industry credibility that a greenfield production directory does not yet possess. |
| **Scope creep risk** | Very high. Talent profiles demand ongoing feature investment: showreel hosting, audition management, breakdown distribution, agent integration. Each feature pulls resources from the core B2B directory proposition. |
| **Verdict** | **Premature and risky for V1.** Every comparable platform that combined talent and crew from launch (or merged them later) has experienced quality dilution and user confusion. CALLSHEET has not yet established its B2B credibility; splitting focus between two fundamentally different user types at launch increases the probability of doing neither well. The Mandy cautionary tale is directly applicable. |

### Option D — Exclude individual talent from V1 but architect for future expansion

| Dimension | Assessment |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Development cost** | Low-moderate. V1 builds the B2B directory with standard service provider profiles. Architecture decisions made now (flexible profile schema, category taxonomy designed for extension, separate "provider type" field) add minimal upfront cost but preserve optionality. |
| **Data protection burden** | Minimal for V1. GDPR compliance for talent deferred until the feature is built. However, the privacy policy and consent architecture should be designed to accommodate future special category data collection. |
| **Competitive positioning** | Strong. Matches The Knowledge for V1 (B2B services + talent agents/CDs). Future talent expansion would differentiate from The Knowledge and compete more directly with 4rfv. Avoids premature competition with Spotlight. |
| **Addressable market impact** | V1 captures the core B2B market (~4,700+ service providers, comparable to 4rfv). Future talent expansion could add £720K–£4.5M ARR once the platform has established credibility and user base. |
| **Scope creep risk** | Controlled. The V1 boundary is clear. The V2+ expansion decision is data-driven — informed by actual user search behaviour, demand signals (e.g., how often users search for "voice-over" or "presenter"), and competitive dynamics. |
| **Verdict** | **Recommended.** This option provides the clearest V1 scope while preserving strategic flexibility. |

---

## The recommended path forward

**Option D is the strongest strategic position**, with one critical amendment to Option B's current V1 plan: the taxonomy should be expanded to ensure all talent-facing B2B services are explicitly included as service provider categories. Specifically:

- **Already included (confirmed):** Casting directors, talent agents (under Business Services → Recruitment)
- **Should be added to V1 taxonomy:** Voice-over agencies, presenter/speaker agencies, extras/supporting artist agencies, stunt coordinators, child performer agencies, model agencies, voice-over production companies
- **Should NOT be included in V1:** Individual performer profiles (actors, voice artists, presenters, extras, stunt performers)

This draws the boundary at **business entities vs. individual performers** — exactly where The Knowledge draws it, with broader category coverage to match 4rfv's scope. A production coordinator searching for "voice-over" on CALLSHEET should find voice-over agencies and production companies, not individual voice artists. This is coherent with the B2B positioning and eliminates the competitive gap without triggering the GDPR, development, or scope creep costs of individual talent profiles.

**Architecture decisions for V1 that preserve V2+ optionality:**

- Design the profile schema with a "provider type" field that can distinguish between business entities and individual freelancers
- Build the category taxonomy as extensible, with the ability to add individual talent subcategories under existing parent categories (e.g., "Voice-Over" parent → "Voice-Over Agencies" and future "Voice-Over Artists")
- Include a search analytics layer from launch to capture demand signals — specifically, track searches that return zero results, which will reveal whether users are searching for individual talent
- Design the consent architecture to accommodate future special category data fields, even if these fields are not exposed in V1

**The decision to add individual talent profiles in V2+ should be triggered by evidence, not assumption.** The signals to watch include: search queries for individual talent returning zero results at significant volume, user feedback requesting talent profiles, competitive moves by 4rfv or The Knowledge into individual talent territory, and demonstrated traction with the B2B service provider model (validating that buyers are actively using the platform for procurement).

**The actual cost of exclusion for V1 is low — provided the B2B talent-services layer is complete.** Buyers in the UK production industry do segment their search behaviour. They go to Spotlight for actors, to specialist agencies for voice and presenters, and to directories like The Knowledge and 4rfv for crew and services. CALLSHEET's credibility depends on comprehensively covering the B2B production services ecosystem — which includes casting directors, talent agents, and voice-over agencies as service businesses. It does not depend on hosting individual actor headshots. The risk is not in excluding talent profiles; it is in leaving gaps where buyers search for legitimate B2B services (like voice-over agencies) and find nothing.
