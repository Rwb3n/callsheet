# B2B directory listing decay: what the data actually shows

**Business listing data decays at roughly 22–30% per year by the most credible estimates, but the evidentiary base is thinner than the industry pretends.** The most-cited figure — 2.1% per month (≈22.5% annually) attributed to MarketingSherpa — is an industry folk statistic whose original study cannot be located. The only traceable primary research, a 2002/2009 business card study by John Coe, found **70.3% of records had at least one changed field in 12 months**, though this includes minor changes like job title promotions. Dun & Bradstreet's analysis of 223 million actual database records found **71% suffered accuracy or completeness gaps**. No published research exists measuring decay rates specific to creative industry or production directories.

---

## The two canonical decay statistics and their shaky provenance

The B2B data quality industry rests on two foundational statistics, both with significant caveats about source quality.

**The "2.1% per month" figure** appears in hundreds of marketing blog posts, HubSpot's interactive Database Decay Simulation tool, and virtually every vendor pitch deck in the data quality space. Every source attributes it to "MarketingSherpa research." However, no one links to the original study, and the actual report title, publication year, sample size, and methodology appear untraceable online. HubSpot popularised it, but HubSpot itself attributes it to MarketingSherpa. This statistic should be treated as **an industry consensus estimate whose original evidence cannot be independently verified**. That said, RevenueBase's 2024 proprietary tracking across millions of B2B records found a baseline decay of **1.5–2.0% per month** (18–24% annualised), which broadly corroborates the range.

**The "70.3% annual decay" figure** traces to John Coe, founder of B2BMarketing.com, who collected over 1,200 business cards from professionals and asked what had changed in the past 12 months. First conducted in 2002 and updated in 2009, this is one of the few studies with traceable methodology. The breakdown by field: **65.8%** had title or job function changes, **42.9%** phone number changes, **41.9%** address changes, **37.3%** email changes, **29.6%** changed companies entirely, and **4.6%** experienced a company name change. The critical caveat: this measures *any* change to *any* field. A person promoted from VP to SVP at the same company counts as "decayed." The figure overstates how many records become genuinely unusable.

D&B's NetProspex "State of Marketing Data" benchmark reports (2013–2015) provide the strongest evidence, based on analysis of actual database records rather than surveys. The 2015 report analysed **223 million records** and found more than 71% had accuracy and completeness gaps, **62% had email deliverability scores rated "questionable or worse"**, 41% lacked a working phone number, and more than 66% were missing revenue and industry data. The 2014 report (61 million records) rated **84% of marketing databases as "barely functional."** NetProspex president Michael Bird stated in Demand Gen Report (2012): "A 2% monthly data decay rate is almost unavoidable for any company with a sizable database."

## Contact detail decay rates broken down by field

Published change rates vary significantly by data type. The BLS Employee Tenure Survey (January 2024) provides the most authoritative anchor point for contact-person decay: **median employee tenure fell to 3.9 years** (lowest since 2002), with private-sector median at **3.5 years**. BLS JOLTS data shows total nonfarm separations running at **3.3% per month (≈39.6% annualised)**, with voluntary quits at **2.1% per month (≈25.2% annualised)**.

For **email addresses**, the best available estimates cluster around **22–30% annual decay**. DealSignal cites 23–30%; the John Coe study found 37.3%; and the MarketingSherpa/HubSpot consensus is 22.5%. RevenueBase's actual tracking data (1.5–2.0% monthly baseline) yields 18–24% annualised. These figures align reasonably well across sources.

For **phone numbers**, data is scarcer. DealSignal estimates **18% annual turnover**; the John Coe study found 42.9% changed or became inactive (a broader definition including disconnected numbers). A-Leads, a B2B data vendor, estimates **1–2% per month** (12–24% annualised). No authoritative telecom industry data was found on business phone number change frequency specifically.

For **physical addresses**, the most concrete data comes from a Hire a Helper analysis of SEC filings: **8.9% of US public companies relocated headquarters** between March 2022 and March 2023, the highest annual total in seven years. For SMEs, no comparable figure exists, but an estimated range of **5–10% annually** is reasonable based on business birth/death rates and general relocation patterns.

For **job titles and contact persons**, the BLS data is definitive: with 22% of workers having ≤1 year tenure at their current employer and monthly separations at 3.3%, **roughly 25–40% of named business contacts will be in different roles within a year**, depending on industry. Leisure and hospitality sees median tenure of just 2.1 years; manufacturing sits at 4.9 years. The John Coe study's 65.8% figure for title/function changes includes internal promotions and is therefore an upper bound.

**Website/URL change rates** represent a genuine data gap. No published percentage exists from any authoritative source. Changes are driven primarily by business closures (~10%), rebranding, and M&A, suggesting an estimated **10–15% annually**, but this lacks hard confirmation.

## UK business dissolution rates paint a clear picture

Companies House data provides the most precise numbers available for UK business churn. In FYE 2025 (April 2024–March 2025), **726,735 companies were dissolved — the highest on record** — against 801,864 incorporations. The dissolution rate calculated as a percentage of the total register reached **13.4%**, up from 12.4% the prior year.

| Financial year ending | Incorporations | Dissolutions | Total register | Dissolution rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2021 | 810,316 | 437,790 | 4,716,126 | 9.3% |
| 2022 | 753,168 | 581,824 | 4,894,356 | 11.9% |
| 2023 | 801,006 | 585,807 | 5,116,743 | 11.4% |
| 2024 | 890,684 | 663,167 | 5,350,759 | 12.4% |
| 2025 | 801,864 | 726,735 | 5,427,787 | 13.4% |

The FYE 2021 figure is artificially low because COVID-19 easements paused strike-off processes for approximately 8 months. The FYE 2025 spike reflects Companies House's new ECCT Act 2023 powers to challenge filings and accelerate strike-offs, plus an incorporation fee increase from £12 to £50 in May 2024. The effective register (excluding companies in dissolution) actually **shrank 0.1%** in FYE 2025 — the first decline recorded. Average age at dissolution has fallen from 5.4 years (FYE 2016) to **4.5 years** (FYE 2025), indicating younger companies are failing faster.

ONS Business Demography data (which counts only VAT/PAYE-registered businesses, a smaller universe of ~2.9 million) shows a death rate of **9.8% in 2024** — the lowest since 2016 — and a birth rate of **11.1%**. Five-year survival rates sit in the **high 30s to low 40s** (the 2019 cohort surviving to 2024 ranged from 30.6% in the West Midlands to 43.5% in the South West). One-year survival for businesses born in 2022 was **92.3%**. Experian's 2023 research found that half of all new UK businesses fail within three years.

The broader UK business population (including unregistered sole traders) stood at **5.69 million at the start of 2025**, of which **99.2% were small businesses** (0–49 employees) and **75% had no employees at all**. Companies House does not break down dissolutions by company size, and ONS does not routinely publish survival rates by employment size band — a significant data gap for anyone modelling micro-business directory decay specifically.

## Creative industry directories: no published decay research exists

**This absence is itself a significant finding.** After exhaustive searching across academic databases, industry reports, government statistics, union publications, and production directory operators, no published research was found that directly measures listing staleness or data decay rates in creative industry, media, production, or freelancer directories. This includes no studies from ProductionHUB, Mandy.com, Stage 32, ProductionBase, The Knowledge, Bectu, IPSE, Creative UK, or ScreenSkills.

However, proxy data strongly suggests creative industry directories face **above-average decay**. DCMS statistics show **268,080 creative industry businesses** in the UK as of March 2024, with **93.4% being microbusinesses** (versus 89.1% UK average). The sector experienced a **net decline of 9.9%** in business numbers between 2019 and 2024. The Information & Communication sector (which overlaps significantly with creative industries) has a **12.8% annual death rate** — the third-highest of any sector. London, where **33.9%** of creative industry businesses cluster, has the lowest regional five-year survival rate.

Staff turnover compounds the problem. The IPA's 2021 Annual Review reported agency staff turnover of **26.8%**. ANA/Forbes data puts advertising industry turnover at approximately **30%** — second only to tourism. For freelancers, IPSE reports **2.07 million UK freelancers** in 2024, but 25% were considering leaving self-employment within 12 months (2022 survey), and research shows **42–50% of freelancers describe their work as intermittent** rather than consistent. The combination of high microbusiness concentration, above-average closure rates, high staff turnover, and freelancer intermittency suggests **annual listing staleness in creative industry directories likely runs at 25–35%**, though this is an inference, not a measured figure.

---

## Conclusion: what's real and what's folklore

The honest summary is that **B2B directory decay research is dominated by a small number of studies, several of which have untraceable provenance, recycled across thousands of vendor blog posts**. The best-evidenced annual decay rate for a typical B2B listing database is **22–30%** — anchored by the D&B NetProspex database audits, corroborated by RevenueBase's tracking data, and broadly consistent with BLS workforce turnover statistics. The oft-cited 70% figure is real but measures "any field changed," not "record became useless."

For UK-specific directory businesses, Companies House dissolution rates of **11–13% annually** set a hard floor: that percentage of company listings will become defunct each year from closures alone, before accounting for address, phone, email, or contact-person changes. ONS data confirms a ~10% annual business death rate for VAT-registered businesses. Creative industry directories almost certainly face higher decay than average, given their concentration of microbusinesses and freelancers, but nobody has measured this directly — a genuine research gap that anyone operating in this space could usefully fill.

Three things that do not exist and should be stated clearly: there is no published annual decay rate study specific to directory platforms (as distinct from CRM/marketing databases); there is no creative-industry-specific directory decay measurement; and the MarketingSherpa study behind the industry's most-cited statistic cannot be located or verified.
